# Narrative Scaffolds Reference

Each scaffold below is a starting structure, not a rigid template. Adapt based on the material, audience, and context. Slides can be added, removed, or reordered — but deviations should be intentional, not accidental.

---

## The Pitch

**Use when:** Asking for money, partnership, or resource commitment. Investors, funders, corporate development, potential partners.

**Core tension:** "Here's a problem worth solving, we're the ones to solve it, and here's what your money unlocks."

**Structure:**

1. **The Problem** — Make it visceral. Quantify the pain. The audience should feel the weight of the status quo before you offer relief.
2. **Why Now** — What changed? Market shift, regulation, technology inflection, cultural moment. If this could have been built five years ago, explain why it wasn't.
3. **The Solution** — What you do, in one sentence. Then show it — demo, screenshot, prototype, customer quote. Don't explain the mechanism yet.
4. **How It Works** — Now go one level deeper. Just enough to prove it's real and defensible. Not a technical deep-dive.
5. **Traction / Proof** — Customers, revenue, growth rate, partnerships, pilots, waitlist. Anything that proves the market is pulling you forward.
6. **Market** — Size the opportunity. TAM/SAM/SOM if appropriate, but avoid fantasy math. Bottom-up beats top-down.
7. **Business Model** — How you make money. Unit economics if you have them.
8. **Team** — Why this team, specifically, is built to win this market. Not résumés — unfair advantages.
9. **The Ask** — How much, what it funds, what milestones it unlocks. Be specific. "We're raising $X to achieve Y by Z."
10. **The Close** — Restate the thesis. One sentence. Leave it ringing.

**Common mistakes:** Burying the ask on slide 15. Leading with the team instead of the problem. Market slides that say "$4.2 trillion" without connecting to your actual serviceable opportunity.

## The Case

**Use when:** Recommending a strategic decision to leadership, board, or cross-functional stakeholders. The audience has authority to approve, reject, or redirect.

**Core tension:** "Here's what the data says, here's the decision it points to, here's the cost of not deciding."

**Structure:**

1. **The Situation** — Current state, in one slide. No history — just where we are right now and why it matters.
2. **The Tension** — What's broken, at risk, or underperforming. Frame as a gap between where we are and where we need to be.
3. **The Options** — Lay out 2-3 credible paths. Not straw men — real alternatives the audience might consider. Brief.
4. **The Recommendation** — Your pick. State it plainly. Then defend it.
5. **The Evidence** — 2-3 slides of proof. Data, benchmarks, precedents, customer input. Each slide makes one point.
6. **The Risk** — What could go wrong. Don't hide it — own it and explain the mitigation. This builds credibility.
7. **The Cost of Inaction** — What happens if we do nothing. Make the status quo uncomfortable.
8. **The Ask** — What you need the room to approve. Budget, timeline, headcount, authority. Be precise.
9. **Next Steps** — Immediate actions, owners, timeline. Show you've already thought past the approval.

**Common mistakes:** Presenting four options when you clearly believe in one (just recommend it). Risk slides that are too vague to be useful. Skipping the cost of inaction.

## The Board Meeting

**Use when:** Presenting to a board of directors, advisory board, or governance body. Quarterly board meetings, special sessions, committee updates. The audience has fiduciary responsibility, limited time, and has (or should have) read the pre-read packet.

**Core tension:** "Here's what the pre-read didn't tell you. Here's where we need your judgment."

**What makes this different from The Case:** The Case asks leadership to approve a recommendation. The Board Meeting briefs people with oversight authority who need to govern, not just decide. They've read the materials. They want to know what you're worried about, what needs their input, and whether management sees around corners. A good board slide starts a conversation. A bad one recites the packet back to people who already read it.

**Structure:**

1. **State of Play** — One slide. Where the organization stands right now in one declarative sentence. Revenue, runway, headcount, or whatever the board tracks as vital signs. No history, no buildup. They know the context — give them the headline.
2. **What Changed** — The material developments since the last meeting. Not a chronological recap — the 2-3 things that shifted the landscape. A new competitor, a regulatory change, a key hire, a lost customer. Each one framed as: what happened, what it means, what we're doing about it.
3. **Performance vs. Plan** — Scorecard slide. Actuals against targets, scannable in five seconds. Green/yellow/red or actual vs. plan. Don't explain the greens — they're fine. Spend your time on the yellows and reds.
4. **Where We're Exposed** — The risks the board needs to know about. Not a generic risk matrix — the specific things keeping management up at night this quarter. For each risk: severity, likelihood, mitigation underway, and what's not yet mitigated. This is where trust gets built. Boards don't punish transparency. They punish surprises.
5. **The Deliberation Item(s)** — The reason this isn't just a consent agenda. The 1-2 topics that need the board's judgment, not just their awareness. Frame each one as a question, not a recommendation (unless a vote is required). Give them the information to discuss, not a conclusion to ratify. Example: "Should we pursue the acquisition at this valuation, or wait for Q3 numbers?"
6. **The Ask** — What specifically requires board action. Approval of a budget, authorization of a transaction, a governance resolution, ratification of a committee recommendation. State it precisely — boards vote on specific language. If nothing requires formal action, say so and skip this slide.
7. **Forward Look** — What's ahead. Key dates, milestones, decisions coming in the next quarter. One slide. The board should leave knowing what's next without having to ask.

**Consent agenda note:** Routine approvals (minutes, committee reports, standard resolutions) should be bundled in the pre-read and handled procedurally. Don't waste slides on items the board will approve without discussion. The deck is for the items that need the room's attention.

**Pre-read integration:** Assume the board has read the packet. Never walk through data that's already in the pre-read. Instead, frame what the data means, where your interpretation differs from what the numbers might suggest at face value, and what questions the data should raise. If you find yourself saying "as you saw in the pre-read" more than once, the deck is redundant.

**Tone:** Measured, direct, no salesmanship. Boards aren't customers — don't pitch them. Present with the confidence of someone who runs the operation and the humility of someone who answers to the room. Avoid jargon the board hasn't adopted. If a board member is hearing a term for the first time in the meeting, you've failed the pre-read.

**Common mistakes:** Reciting the pre-read back to people who already read it. Burying risks in an appendix nobody opens. Presenting "deliberation items" that are actually pre-decided — if you've already made the call, say so and ask for ratification, don't perform a discussion you've already had internally. Slides that require the presenter to be in the room to make sense — board decks get circulated to members who couldn't attend.

## The Keynote

**Use when:** Speaking to a conference, public audience, or large internal gathering. The goal is to shift thinking, not approve a budget.

**Core tension:** "Here's what you think you know. Here's what's actually true. Here's what to do about it."

**Structure:**

1. **The Opening Scene** — A concrete, sensory moment that drops the audience into the world. Not a statistic — a story. 15 seconds to hook.
2. **The Conventional Wisdom** — What "everyone knows" about this topic. State it fairly. The audience should nod.
3. **The Crack** — Where the conventional wisdom breaks. An anomaly, a data point, a story that doesn't fit. This is your pivot.
4. **The Reframe** — Your thesis. The new way to see this. One sentence, said clearly. This is the slide the audience remembers.
5. **Beat 1: Evidence** — First proof point for your reframe. Story, data, or example. One idea per slide.
6. **Beat 2: Evidence** — Second proof point. Different type of evidence than Beat 1 for variety.
7. **Beat 3: Evidence** — Third proof point. By now, the case should feel inevitable.
8. **The Implication** — So what? If your reframe is true, what changes? For the audience, for the industry, for the work.
9. **The Charge** — What you're asking the audience to do, think, or believe differently. Close with energy and specificity. Don't trail off.

**Common mistakes:** Opening with "Thank you for having me" or "I'm excited to be here." More than three evidence beats (diminishing returns). Closing with "Questions?" instead of a charge.

## The Report-Out

**Use when:** Updating stakeholders on progress — quarterly reviews, project updates, campaign results. The audience knows the context; they want the delta.

**Core tension:** "Here's what happened, here's what it means, here's what we're doing next."

**Structure:**

1. **The Headline** — One slide. What's the single most important thing the audience needs to know? Lead with it. Don't make them wait.
2. **Scorecard** — Key metrics vs. targets. Green/yellow/red or actual vs. plan. One slide, scannable in 5 seconds.
3. **What Worked** — 1-2 slides on wins. Be specific — name the action, name the result. Not "marketing performed well" but "the email sequence drove 340 qualified leads at $12 CPL."
4. **What Didn't** — 1-2 slides on misses. Same specificity. Own it without wallowing. Pair each miss with the diagnosis.
5. **The Insight** — One non-obvious learning from the period. The thing the data revealed that changes how you'll operate going forward.
6. **Next Period Plan** — What you're doing next, based on what you learned. 2-3 priorities, each tied to a metric.
7. **The Ask** — What you need from this audience. Approval, resources, air cover, a decision. If you don't need anything, skip this slide — but think hard about whether that's really true.

**Common mistakes:** Spending 20 slides on what happened and one slide on what it means. Hiding misses. Presenting plans that don't connect to the lessons.

## The Workshop

**Use when:** Teaching a framework, skill, or methodology. The audience is there to learn and apply, not to decide.

**Core tension:** "Here's what you can't do yet. Here's the framework. Now do it."

**Structure:**

1. **The Gap** — Show the audience what they're missing. A before/after, a failure mode they'll recognize, a problem they've hit. Make it personal.
2. **The Framework** — Introduce the model, method, or tool. Name it. Visualize it if possible. Keep the initial explanation to one slide.
3. **Component 1** — First element of the framework. Explain with an example, not in the abstract.
4. **Component 2** — Second element. Different example. Build.
5. **Component 3** — Third element. By now, the audience should see the whole shape.
6. **The Demo / Worked Example** — Walk through a complete application of the framework. Real scenario, real data if possible.
7. **Common Mistakes** — Where people go wrong when they first try this. Name 2-3 traps.
8. **The Exercise** — If live: give them something to do. If async: give them a template, checklist, or first step they can take immediately.
9. **The Close** — Restate the framework in one sentence. Point them to resources. End with what mastery looks like.

**Common mistakes:** Teaching in the abstract without examples. Frameworks with more than 5 components (simplify or layer). No exercise or application moment.

## The Close

**Use when:** Final-stage sales presentation, deal room, or decision meeting. The audience has context — they've seen earlier materials. This is about commitment.

**Core tension:** "You've seen the evidence. Here's why now. Here's the next step."

**Structure:**

1. **The Recap** — One slide. Restate the problem and your solution in the audience's language (not yours). Prove you listened.
2. **The Proof Stack** — 2-3 slides of results, case studies, or testimonials most relevant to this specific buyer. Tailored, not generic.
3. **The Differentiator** — Why you and not the alternative. One slide. Be specific about what they'd lose by choosing differently.
4. **The Objection** — Name the biggest concern in the room. You know what it is. Address it directly. Don't wait for them to raise it.
5. **The Terms** — Pricing, timeline, scope, what's included. Clear and scannable. No surprises.
6. **The Next Step** — One specific action. "Sign the SOW by Friday" or "Schedule the pilot kickoff for next week." Not "let us know." A specific, low-friction action.

**Common mistakes:** Re-pitching from scratch (they already know). Generic case studies that don't match the buyer's context. Ending with "we'd love to work with you" instead of a concrete next step.
